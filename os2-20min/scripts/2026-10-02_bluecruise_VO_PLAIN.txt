Two Ford Mustang Mach-Es. Same hands-free system. Eight days apart. Both drove into stopped cars at more than 70 miles an hour, and neither one touched the brakes. Three people died. In one of those crashes, the car's own sensors detected the stopped vehicle ahead, and it still didn't brake. Federal investigators spent two years finding out why. If you've ever used hands-free driving, or even adaptive cruise control, what they found applies to the car you drive.

February 24th, 2024. 9:48 at night. Interstate 10, San Antonio. A 2022 Ford Mustang Mach-E is doing 74 miles an hour in the center lane. Its driver isn't steering. The car is. Ford calls it BlueCruise: hands-free highway driving.

Ahead, in that same lane, a 1999 Honda CR-V is sitting completely still. The road is straight. It's lit. Investigators later measured a clear line of sight of about 1,300 feet. A driver ahead of the Mach-E saw the Honda and swerved around it. The Mach-E didn't brake. It didn't steer. Neither did its driver.

Eight days later, a second Mach-E did the same thing on Interstate 95 in Philadelphia. What investigators found wasn't one broken part. It was a chain. A car that detected a stopped vehicle and still didn't brake. A blind spot the automaker says it already knew about. A driver camera that could be fooled. Speed settings that let drivers cruise 20 over the limit. Crashes the company didn't even know had happened. And almost no federal rules for any of it.

First, what BlueCruise actually is. In Ford's own how-to video, it's "hands-free highway driving that helps make driving less stressful and more enjoyable." It works in what Ford calls Blue Zones, pre-mapped stretches covering about 97 percent of controlled-access highways. Turn it on, and the car handles accelerating, braking and steering, "so you can let go and relax while continuing to watch the road."

That last part is the catch. BlueCruise is what engineers call Level 2, partial automation. The human is still the driver, responsible for spotting anything the system misses. To make sure you're watching, Ford mounts a camera on the steering column that tracks where your eyes are pointed. Look away too long, and it warns you.

Systems like this aren't rare anymore. At the NTSB's public meeting on these crashes, a board member noted that more than half of all new vehicles sold in the U.S. now come with Level 2 technology. There's also a question of what these systems actually buy you in safety. NTSB cited an Insurance Institute for Highway Safety study that compared cars with Level 2 systems to similar cars with only basic crash-avoidance features. It found no lower crash rates, and concluded these systems work mainly as convenience features, not safety enhancements. So the question investigators had to answer goes far beyond Ford. What happens when the system meets something it can't handle, and the person who's supposed to catch it isn't looking?

Back to Interstate 10. The Mach-E's driver was heading from San Antonio to Houston, following navigation to a charging station. He wasn't impaired; investigators ruled that out. He'd owned the car since new and driven it more than 11,000 miles. What the car's driver camera recorded was a pattern: glances down at the center screen, quick looks back at the road, then back to the screen.

Ford's own data showed the system warned him twice: 29 seconds before impact, and again 8 seconds before. Combined with the camera data, investigators found he was looking away from the road for at least 14 of the last 30 seconds. His last glance at the road came two-tenths of a second before the crash.

The Honda wasn't invisible. Police body-camera video showed its headlights on and a rear hazard light lit. Lab analysis of the taillight bulbs showed at least some rear lighting was on at impact. Why the Honda had stopped in a live lane was never determined. But toxicology found its driver's blood alcohol at more than four times the legal limit, which investigators said could have contributed.

The driver who swerved told investigators they were preparing to take an exit, and NTSB said that's why they were focused on the road. NTSB also said the Mach-E's driver had roughly 11 to 12 seconds to see the Honda and react. He never did. Nothing in the data shows the car tried, either. And the Mach-E had a camera and a radar pointed straight down that lane. So why didn't the car brake? The answer was in a place many owners never read.

Eight days later. March 3rd, 3:16 in the morning. Northbound Interstate 95 in Philadelphia, inside a long-term construction zone where the speed limit had been cut from 55 to 45. A Hyundai Elantra and a Toyota Prius were stopped in the left lane, and their drivers were outside or getting out.

A Mach-E came up behind them in hands-free BlueCruise at about 72 miles an hour, 27 over the work-zone limit. It hit the Elantra without braking, pushing it into the Prius, and a passing Corolla was struck too. Both stopped drivers died.

This time, the Mach-E's driver was impaired. A hospital test taken hours later, worked backward, put blood alcohol at roughly 0.14 to 0.19, far above Pennsylvania's 0.08. Cannabis was also detected, though investigators couldn't say whether it played a role.

Then there's the single still image the driver camera saved just before impact. It shows the driver upright, facing forward, eyes open, wrists on top of the steering wheel, and the left hand holding what investigators say looks like a phone. As far as the car's camera was concerned, this driver was watching the road.

The sight line here was shorter, about 524 feet around a curve. At 72 miles an hour, that's about five seconds to react. At the posted 45, investigators calculated, it would have been almost eight. And the car? It issued a forward collision warning two-tenths of a second before impact. Far too late to matter.

Here's the detail that changes how you read both crashes. In Philadelphia, the Mach-E's camera-radar system did detect a stopped vehicle ahead, 0.4 seconds before impact. It didn't brake. NTSB says that's because the system's confidence that the stationary vehicle was actually a threat was too low to trigger automatic emergency braking.

That wasn't a glitch. Ford told investigators it does not expect the current generation of these radar-and-camera systems to reliably detect stationary vehicles as a hazard at highway speeds at night. NTSB Vice Chairman Michael Graham said Ford told NTSB it knew these limitations before the investigation began. And how did Ford tell owners? Through the owner's manual. The Mach-E's manual says its collision warning and emergency braking can detect stationary vehicles from 3 miles an hour up to the vehicle's top speed, and cautions that performance can degrade for many reasons, including nighttime. Homendy asked whether owners really understand those limits. An NTSB staff member answered: "Many engineers don't, so I wouldn't expect the owners to."

Why would a system that can see a car choose not to brake for it? An NTSB automation expert explained it this way. Detecting an object on the road isn't the hard part. Deciding that it's a vehicle, in your lane, and that it's a real threat: that's hard. Early collision warning systems flagged overpasses as hazards every single time. And a false emergency stop at highway speed can be dangerous on its own. Ford and NTSB described it as an industry-wide problem. At higher speeds, the system's confidence that a stopped object is a threat never gets high enough to trigger emergency braking.

The testing didn't catch it, either. The 2022 Mach-E did well in existing emergency-braking tests run by NHTSA and Europe's safety rating program. But NHTSA's stopped-vehicle test tops out at 45 miles an hour for the warning and 25 for automatic braking. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety tested braking at 25, too, and only recently moved up to 40. None of those tests looked like a dark interstate at 74.

NTSB's formal finding: the conditions of both crashes, a stopped car, highway speed and darkness, were likely outside what the Mach-E's collision-avoidance systems could handle. Graham put it bluntly. If his car had automatic emergency braking, he said, he would assume it could detect a stopped vehicle in the middle of a well-lit highway. He called these crashes "a wake-up call to the manufacturers and the regulators alike."

Which puts all the weight on the backup: the human. That's what the driver camera is there to protect. Here's how Ford's version works. If it sees your eyes off the road for more than five seconds, it starts escalating warnings. For comparison, NHTSA's voluntary distraction guidelines are built around two seconds. Europe's safety rating program requires an alert after three. At 70 miles an hour, five seconds covers more than 500 feet. Almost two football fields.

Investigators found two bigger gaps. First, even a very brief glance back at the road resets the timer. And the system only looks at the last five seconds, so it never adds up a pattern of constant glancing away. That's how the San Antonio driver could spend at least 14 of the last 30 seconds looking elsewhere and get only two warnings.

Second, the camera can't tell the road from something held in front of it. NTSB recreated the Philadelphia driver's position in an identical Mach-E, with a test driver holding a phone up near the top of the wheel. When he looked at the phone, the system consistently logged his gaze as being on the windshield.

And here's the uncomfortable part. By American standards, this is one of the better systems. NTSB called Ford's camera monitoring a major improvement over systems that only check whether your hands are on the wheel. Its five-second threshold is among the shortest in the U.S. industry. As one NTSB expert put it, the mere presence of a camera-based monitor "does not inoculate against driver disengagement."

Then there's how these systems are sold. At the meeting, NTSB Chair Jennifer Homendy played a Ford BlueCruise ad with the line "Hands-free highway driving for a hands-on life." As she described it, it shows a driver pretending to conduct an orchestra, eyes partly closed, kids in the back. Ford's manual tells drivers to keep their eyes on the road at all times. Homendy called the ad "irresponsible," and said the problem isn't only Ford's.

The third link in the chain is speed. Hands-free BlueCruise can be set as high as 80 miles an hour. Ford also offers an intelligent cruise mode that reads the speed limit, but BlueCruise doesn't require it. And even when it's on, it lets drivers set a cruise speed up to 20 miles an hour over the limit.

In Philadelphia, intelligent cruise wasn't on. But NTSB pointed out that even if it had been, the 20-mile tolerance would have allowed about 65 in that 45 work zone. The car's map still listed the old 55 limit there. During the meeting, Graham walked through the logic, and staff confirmed it: as far as speed goes, in a Blue Zone that becomes a work zone, hands-free BlueCruise can still be set to 80. And in hands-on mode, which isn't limited to Blue Zones, the adaptive cruise can be set to 113 miles an hour.

One more setting: drivers can switch off automatic emergency braking and still use BlueCruise. That wasn't a factor in either crash, but NTSB listed it as a design deficiency. And speed mattered. NTSB found that if the Philadelphia Mach-E had been going the posted 45, its collision warning and emergency braking would have been more likely to alert the driver sooner or lessen the crash. In the official probable cause, the board named Ford's "inadequate integration" of speed management with BlueCruise, which permitted excessive speed, including in a work zone, as a contributing factor.

Then investigators asked a simpler question: when did Ford find out about these crashes? For San Antonio, Ford learned nearly two weeks later, from a post on an online Mach-E owners' forum that Ford staff monitored. For Philadelphia, it took about a month. Pennsylvania State Police had called an NTSB investigator with questions about BlueCruise. When NTSB contacted Ford, Ford didn't know the crash had happened.

The data told a similar story. In San Antonio, the BlueCruise module didn't record the crash at all. Nothing on what its sensors saw, no forward-camera images. In Philadelphia, it kept just two seconds of pre-crash data. Ford's telematics missed the final minutes of the San Antonio trip and all of the Philadelphia trip, and unlike some automakers' systems, they don't send the company a direct crash signal like an airbag deployment. None of this broke a rule. Federal event-data-recorder rules don't require any driver-assistance data, not even whether a system like BlueCruise was switched on.

Even the car's automatic emergency call didn't get through. In Philadelphia, Ford's 911 Assist, which relies on the driver's cell phone, started dialing about two seconds after the crash. After several attempts over about seven minutes, the call was marked complete, but Pennsylvania State Police had no record of receiving it. Troopers learned about the crash from witnesses. NTSB found the emergency response itself was timely.

That gap shows up in the federal crash database. Since 2021, NHTSA has required automakers to report crashes where a Level 2 system was engaged within 30 seconds of impact. At NTSB's last review, Ford had reported 35. Tesla had reported more than 2,400, about 85 percent of everything in the database. Homendy was careful here. That doesn't mean Tesla's system is the problem; Tesla's cars tell Tesla about crashes, so Tesla reports more. NTSB's conclusion: the database likely doesn't reflect the full extent of Level 2 crashes in the U.S.

Which leads to the biggest gap of all: rules. There are no federal performance standards for systems like BlueCruise. None for driver cameras. None requiring this data to be recorded. The European Union already requires a number of these parameters to be recorded. NTSB first asked U.S. regulators to define this kind of data after investigating a fatal partial-automation crash from 2016. Those recommendations have now been closed as "unacceptable."

In 2020, NTSB asked NHTSA and SAE International, the engineering group that defined the levels of driving automation, to develop performance standards for driver monitoring. According to NTSB, SAE never formally responded. NHTSA told investigators SAE wasn't interested, and that it would pursue the research on its own. That standard still doesn't exist. Homendy called SAE's lack of a written response "pretty shameful." And an NTSB expert said NHTSA's primary focus has been on higher levels of automation, like robotaxis.

There is one rule on the way. In 2024, NHTSA finalized a standard requiring automatic emergency braking on new passenger vehicles, including avoiding stopped vehicles at up to 62 miles an hour, engaging at up to 90, and working at night on low beams. NTSB says it addresses these crash conditions. But the compliance date is September 2029. According to NTSB, NHTSA said only one automaker may meet it today, and automakers have raised concerns about meeting it.

So who's responsible? Ford's response, as reported by Autoline, pointed to alcohol impairment as a key cause of the crashes. That's part of the record. One Mach-E driver was impaired, and so was the driver of the stopped Honda. NTSB's probable causes put the drivers first: distraction, impairment and overreliance on automation. But in San Antonio, the board also listed the system's inability to detect and respond to the stopped car as a contributing cause.

NTSB issued six new recommendations. To Ford: require automatic emergency braking to be on whenever BlueCruise is engaged, use tighter speed tolerances, and upgrade the driver camera to catch accumulated glances and phones held in view. To NHTSA: require Level 2 vehicles to record system data and notify automakers of crashes, and require driver monitoring that closes those same gaps. To the Department of Transportation: write guidelines for how these systems should be built, including maximum speeds.

NHTSA has its own investigation. It opened in April 2024 and was upgraded in January 2025 to an engineering analysis covering about 129,000 Mach-Es. NHTSA said its early work found "limitations in the detection of stationary vehicles in certain conditions," and that "system performance may be limited when there is poor visibility due to insufficient illumination." As of our research, we could not find a public record of how that investigation has been resolved.

So what do you do with this if you drive a car with hands-free or adaptive cruise? Start with the limitation Ford itself described to investigators: a stopped vehicle, at highway speed, at night, is exactly the situation these systems may not handle. Treat hands-free as hands-free, not eyes-free. If your car offers speed-limit-based cruise, use it, and don't switch off automatic emergency braking. And remember the math from Philadelphia: the same sight line gave about five seconds at 72, and almost eight at 45.

What we still don't know: how NHTSA's investigation ends, whether Ford has adopted NTSB's recommendations, how newer versions of BlueCruise handle stopped cars at night, and whether the 2029 braking rule arrives on time.

But one detail from San Antonio sticks. The Mach-E had a radar, a camera and about 1,300 feet of open road. The one driver who got around that Honda did it with a quick lane change, because that driver was watching the road, looking for an exit. Hands-free can take your hands off the wheel. It can't take your eyes off the road.
