### 07:12 — THE CAR SAW IT. WHY DIDN'T IT BRAKE?

**[07:12]**
[NARRATION] Here's the detail that changes how you read both crashes. In Philadelphia, the Mach-E's camera-radar system did detect a stopped vehicle ahead, 0.4 seconds before impact. It didn't brake. NTSB says that's because the system's confidence that the stationary vehicle was actually a threat was too low to trigger automatic emergency braking.
[VISUAL] Stylized sensor view at night: radar returns and camera bounding boxes over a dark road. A gray box appears around a stopped car shape, labeled LOW CONFIDENCE, with a clock reading 0.4 SECONDS. The brake indicator stays dark.

**[07:34]**
[NARRATION] That wasn't a glitch. Ford told investigators it does not expect the current generation of these radar-and-camera systems to reliably detect stationary vehicles as a hazard at highway speeds at night. The Mach-E's manual says its collision warning and emergency braking can detect stationary vehicles from 3 miles an hour up to the vehicle's top speed, and cautions that performance can degrade for many reasons, including nighttime. Homendy asked whether owners really understand those limits. An NTSB staff member answered: "Many engineers don't, so I wouldn't expect the owners to."

**[08:24]**
