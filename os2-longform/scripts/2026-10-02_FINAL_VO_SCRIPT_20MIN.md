# After Takata: The Airbag Crisis America Never Finished (20-min cut)

## 00:00 Cold Open
Eight drivers. Killed in crashes investigators described as otherwise survivable — not by the crash itself, but by the airbag in front of them.

That was the count federal investigators gave in January 2026. Three months earlier, reports put it at six. Three months later, NHTSA's initial findings, as reported, said ten.

And the part involved wasn't installed at a factory. The company linked to it says it doesn't even sell in the United States. And the federal agency investigating it says the parts were likely brought into the country illegally — by importers it has not identified.

If you drive a used car — especially one that has been in a crash before — this is about you.

Because this isn't the only airbag problem on American roads right now. It's one of three. And the free tool most people trust to check their car can only show you some of them.

 The second: in 2023, the federal government demanded a recall of tens of millions of airbag inflators. The company that made them said no. The third, many people assume is over. In February 2026, one automaker told the owners of about two hundred and twenty-five thousand vehicles, in plain words: do not drive.

Three different failures. One part of the car you never see, never service, and never think about.

In the next twenty minutes, we'll take all three apart — what we were able to verify, what we weren't, and, by the end, the specific steps that tell you whether any of this applies to the car in your driveway.

Let's start with the number that kept moving.

## 01:58 The Number That Kept Moving
October 2025. Federal regulators open an investigation into a Chinese manufacturer of airbag inflators, known by three letters: DTN. By then, according to reporting at the time, its inflators had been tied to six deaths. Regulators said they "likely would have been illegally imported" as aftermarket equipment — usually installed after the original airbag had already been used in a prior crash.

Not original equipment. Installed later — after a crash — by someone fixing the car.

January 2026. NHTSA issues an urgent alert to used-car owners. Now the count is eight drivers killed and two seriously injured. And the agency's language shifts. As quoted by reporters, NHTSA wrote: "Whoever is bringing them into the country and installing them is putting American families in danger."

That reads less like the language of a defect notice — and more like the language of a supply chain investigators can't see.

April 2026. NHTSA publishes initial findings. As reported at the time, the count was ten deaths and two serious injuries, across a dozen crashes, over roughly three years. The agency called the inflators "substandard," said they were likely imported "by unknown importers," and said it was considering a nationwide ban on their sale and import.

Six. Eight. Ten. In roughly six months. We can document that count through April — not after. If you're watching this later, the number on NHTSA's website may be higher.

So what is inside that steering wheel that can turn protection into danger?

## 03:46 What's Actually Inside an Airbag
Most people picture an airbag as a fabric bag. The part that matters here is the one you don't see: the inflator.

An inflator is, essentially, a small gas generator. Chemicals inside ignite during a collision to fill the airbag almost instantly — in the words of one person NBC interviewed, "the blink of an eye." A sealed metal container has to hold all that energy and send it into the bag.

When it works, it's invisible. NHTSA's ads put it simply: "Air bags are designed to protect you." When it fails, the container itself becomes the hazard. NHTSA has described an inflator that "may rupture when the vehicle's airbag is commanded to deploy, causing metal debris to be forcefully ejected into the passenger compartment." For recalled Takata airbags, the agency says metal fragments can fly "at 200 miles per hour."

We are not going to describe injuries. You don't need graphic detail to understand the problem.

Here's the framework for this whole story. An inflator can fail for three different reasons.

One: the chemistry. The material inside changes over time. That's Takata.

Two: the container. Installed at the factory by a known manufacturer — and the government says some can rupture. That's ARC.

Three: the chain of custody. The part was never supposed to be in the car at all. That's DTN.

Chemistry. Container. Chain of custody. And the third is the hardest — because the question "whose part is this?" doesn't have an answer anyone will sign their name to.

## 05:36 Why the Replacement Part Matters
An airbag only works once. After a crash, if the car is going back on the road, the deployed parts have to be replaced. That moment is where this story lives.

According to NBC's January 2026 report, investigators say these inflators most often ended up in cars after a previous crash — when the original airbag was replaced with a cheap substitute. And according to NHTSA, all of the known deaths at that point involved Chevrolet Malibus and Hyundai Sonatas "with salvage or rebuilt titles" — cars that suffered serious damage and were later put back on the road.

Follow the chain. A car is wrecked. The airbags deploy. Someone repairs it, and somewhere in that repair, someone chooses a part. If it's a substandard inflator, the car can still leave the shop looking perfect. As NBC put it, drivers "often have no idea what airbags are inside their car until it's too late."

Where did the parts come from? One person interviewed in that report said they were being sold, "we think, through online marketplaces." Notice the words "we think." A belief, not a mapped supply chain.

At the factory, there's a bill of materials. In a repair, a part can come from almost anywhere. And the person most often left holding the risk is the next owner, with every reason to believe the airbag in front of them is the one the car came with.

So who made these parts?

## 07:21 DTN
The company is based in China's Jilin Province — reported as "Jilin Province Detiannuo" — and everyone shortens it the same way: DTN.

According to reporting in January 2026, the company's homepage reads: "We do not do business with the United States, and any of our products are prohibited from being sold to the United States." NBC said it reached out for comment and had not heard back.

So: a company saying its products don't belong here, and a U.S. safety agency saying they're in American cars, linked to deaths. Both can be true. Products can arrive through other hands. What the record we could verify doesn't show is how each part traveled — and we're not going to fill that gap with guesses.

Now the government's own words: "likely illegally imported into the United States by unknown importers." Likely. Unknown. Those two words are the whole problem. There's no company at the border end of this chain the government can point to and say: you — recall these.

Behind the numbers are real people. Reporting in October 2025 described a 22-year-old mother of two in Florida. According to a lawsuit her family filed against DTN, she was traveling at about 30 miles per hour when an airbag with a DTN inflator exploded. That's an allegation in a lawsuit, not a court finding we could verify — but it describes exactly the kind of crash airbags exist to make survivable.

So why can't the recall system just find these parts?

## 09:10 Why the Recall System Can't Simply Find It
Here's how it's supposed to work, in NHTSA's own words: "Use the VIN lookup tool at NHTSA.gov. Or download the SaferCar app. Repairs are free." Another NHTSA ad sums it up in one sentence: "Your vehicle is either affected by safety recalls, or it isn't."

Notice the shape of that system. It starts with a vehicle — a specific VIN. With a factory defect, the automaker knows which supplier built which part and can tie it to a list of VINs. That list is what makes the free fix work.

Now apply that to DTN. No automaker installed it. The company says it doesn't sell here. The importers are unknown. The part went in during a repair. So which list of VINs would a recall be built on? Reporting cited an estimated ten thousand inflators under investigation — but how many are actually in American cars was unclear.

That's why NHTSA's advice doesn't sound like a normal recall notice. As quoted in January, the agency urged owners and buyers to "learn their vehicle's history and ensure the vehicle has genuine air bag inflators" — and to get a history report and an inspection if the car was in a previous crash with airbag deployment. That isn't "check your VIN." It's "go find out yourself."

And the ban? We could not review a final order, so we won't describe its legal status as fact. But any ban is aimed at the future. On its own, it doesn't reach a part installed last year.

So what happens when the company is known, the part was installed at the factory, and the government puts its demand in writing? That should be the easy case.

It wasn't.

## 11:14 ARC and Delphi: The Company That Said No
May 2023. In a letter, NHTSA demands that Tennessee-based ARC Automotive recall 67 million airbag inflators, saying they posed "an unreasonable risk of death or injury" and could rupture. According to ABC News, at least twelve automakers had used ARC components — a recall that could ultimately affect nearly a quarter of the cars on American roads.

And ARC said no.

According to ABC, the company said any problems "resulted from random one-off manufacturing anomalies that were properly addressed with individual recalls." As NBC reported it, ARC argued that "just seven incidents in the U.S. over a decade and a half do not support a finding that a systemic and prevalent defect exists across this population."

That deserves to be stated fairly: across tens of millions of parts, that is statistically rare. NHTSA acknowledged incidents were rare — but said the ones that occurred were severe, including a driver's death in Michigan in 2021.

Rare, but severe. That's the whole fight. Recall tens of millions of parts, and you'd better be right that the failures share a cause. Don't, and every future failure becomes one you were warned about.

One automaker didn't wait. General Motors voluntarily recalled nearly one million vehicles with ARC inflators — certain 2014 through 2017 Chevrolet Traverse, GMC Acadia and Buick Enclave models — "out of an abundance of caution."

September 2023. NHTSA moves forward, and the number changes: 52 million inflators, made by ARC and by Delphi. According to Autoline, about 11 million of them were Delphi's, made under a licensing agreement with ARC. The sources we reviewed don't explain the drop from 67 to 52 million, so we won't guess. ARC still disputed any defect.

And here the public record gets thin. We could not confirm that a final order was ever issued. We're not saying it never happened. We're saying we couldn't document it.

Your VIN check can show GM's recall. It can't show a recall that doesn't exist.

And then there's the third case — where the recall happened, nobody is arguing, and the fix is free.

And it still isn't finished.

## 13:46 Takata: The Recall That Never Finished
NHTSA puts it plainly: "There are millions of defective Takata air bags on the road that need repair." The problem is the chemical inside — and "the greatest risk is in older vehicles, especially vehicles driven in hot, humid areas."

So the warnings escalated. In May 2023, a "do not drive" warning went out for about 90,000 BMW vehicles built between 2000 and 2006 — cars already under a Takata recall. Parts, repair and towing: all free.

In Texas, WFAA reported that Carfax had identified the state as leading the country in vehicles still carrying recalled Takata airbags. One viewer's 2013 car still showed "repair not yet available" in 2025. Nothing moved — until he filed a formal complaint with NHTSA and asked the manufacturer, in writing, for an explanation. Then the dealer took the car in and gave him a loaner, at no cost. For one owner, persistence closed the gap.

February 2026. Stellantis issues a "do not drive" warning for about 225,000 vehicles with unrepaired Takata airbags — model years 2003 through 2016, including certain Dodge Rams, Chargers, Challengers, Durangos, Chrysler 300s and Jeep Wranglers. One TV report noted at least 28 people have died because of these airbags.

And that's one company's share. That same week, WPXI reported a Carfax estimate of about five million cars nationwide still unrepaired — with California, Texas and Florida at the top. Hot, humid states. Exactly where NHTSA says the risk is greatest.

So with Takata, the recall exists, the fix is free, the lookup works — and millions of cars, by that estimate, still carry the defective part. The failure isn't in the engineering anymore. It's in the last steps: reaching the owner, having the part, and getting the car to the dealer.

And those older, cheaper cars sit in the same market where cheap replacement parts end up after crashes. Different failures. Often, the same buyers.

## 16:05 The Systemic Question
Put the three side by side. Takata: the assumption that broke is that once a recall exists, people will get it done. ARC: that a regulator's conclusion quickly becomes a fix. DTN: that every part in a car can be traced back to someone responsible for it.

What they share: the critical information — is this part safe, has it been fixed, where did it come from — doesn't travel with the car.

One counterpoint, because it matters for your safety: none of this means airbags are dangerous. The failures in this story involve specific parts. Disabling an airbag is not the lesson. Knowing which part is in your car is the lesson.

## 16:54 What We Still Don't Know
Here's what we could not verify. The DTN count after April 2026. The legal status of the proposed ban. How many DTN inflators are in American cars, or exactly how they were sold. Whether the ARC and Delphi case ever reached a final order, and why the number fell from 67 to 52 million. And the five million unrepaired Takata cars is an estimate, not a measurement. For your car specifically, check NHTSA's website — and ask a qualified mechanic.

## 17:29 What You Can Actually Do
Here's what to do. None of it requires you to open or touch anything yourself.

One: run the recall check at NHTSA.gov with your VIN or license plate, and sign up for recall alerts.

Two: if there's an open Takata recall — especially a "do not drive" warning — don't drive the car until it's repaired. The repair is free.

Three: know your car's history. If you don't, get a history report. If it's been in a crash with airbag deployment — especially with a salvage or rebuilt title — have it inspected by a reputable mechanic or dealership.

Four: let a professional check who manufactured the airbag. Never open airbag components yourself.

Five: if a DTN inflator is found, don't drive the car until the airbag is replaced with one that meets U.S. safety standards — and report it to NHTSA. According to reporting on the January warning, drivers who find one were also advised to contact Homeland Security or the FBI.

Six: if your fix shows "not yet available," file a complaint with NHTSA and write to the manufacturer.

And if your brand was reportedly linked to ARC or Delphi inflators and your VIN check shows nothing, keep checking — with alerts on.

## 18:58 Final: The Part You Only Test Once
An airbag exists for one idea: crashes "that you should walk away from," in the words of one person NBC interviewed.

In this story, that promise breaks three ways. A chemical that changes as the car ages. A container the government says can rupture and the manufacturer says is sound. And a replacement part nobody will stand behind.

NHTSA's ad says your vehicle "is either affected by safety recalls, or it isn't." For Takata, that works. For ARC and Delphi, we couldn't document a final decision. For a part installed after a crash, it barely applies — because the lookup is built around the VIN, and this part went in after the car left the factory.

What connects them is information that existed somewhere — and never reached the person in the driver's seat.

So tonight: check your VIN. Learn your car's history. And if the airbags have ever gone off, find out what's in the steering wheel now.

Because an airbag is something you only ever get to test once.
