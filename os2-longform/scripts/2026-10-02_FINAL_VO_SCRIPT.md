## 06:55 What's Actually Inside an Airbag

Most people picture an airbag as a balloon. A fabric bag that inflates. That's the part you see.

The part that matters for this story is the one you don't see. It's called the inflator.

An inflator is, essentially, a small gas generator. Inside it are chemicals that ignite during a collision to rapidly fill the airbag with gas. NHTSA, explaining the Takata recall, describes the problem in exactly those terms: "The problem is with a chemical inside the airbag that works to inflate the airbag during a crash."

And it has to happen fast. As one person NBC interviewed put it, the airbag "has to deploy really quickly — the blink of an eye."

Think about what that asks of a small metal part. A sealed container has to go from inert to full of rapidly expanding gas almost instantly — and then send that gas, in a controlled way, into a fabric bag in front of a person's face and chest. The bag is the cushion. The container is what keeps all that energy pointed where it's supposed to go.

When it works, that container is invisible. NHTSA's own public service ads say it simply: "Air bags are designed to protect you."

When it doesn't work, the container itself becomes the hazard.

NHTSA has described that failure, for one group of inflators, as an inflator that "may rupture when the vehicle's airbag is commanded to deploy, causing metal debris to be forcefully ejected into the passenger compartment." For recalled Takata airbags, the agency says metal fragments can be sent "flying at 200 miles per hour."

For the DTN inflators, one person interviewed in NBC's January report described it this way: in a crash — "even a minor crash" — these substandard inflators were "deploying and overpressuring," sending metal toward the driver.

We are not going to describe injuries in this video. You don't need graphic detail to understand the problem: a device meant to put a soft barrier between you and the steering wheel instead sends hard metal into the cabin.

Now here's the framework that ties this entire story together. An inflator can fail for different reasons — and each reason exposes a different weak point in the system around it.

One: the chemistry. The material inside behaves differently over time than it did on the day it was built. That's the Takata story. NHTSA says the greatest risk is in older vehicles, especially vehicles driven in hot, humid areas.

Two: the container. The inflator was built by a known manufacturer and installed at the factory — and the government says some of them can rupture. The manufacturer says those were isolated anomalies. That's the ARC story, and as you'll see, it turned into a standoff.

Three: the chain of custody. The part was never supposed to be in the car at all. It went in after the fact, from a source investigators say they haven't identified. That's DTN.

Chemistry. Container. Chain of custody.

And the third one is, in a way, the hardest. With the first two, everyone at least knows whose part it is. With the third, the question "whose part is this?" doesn't have an answer anyone will sign their name to.

To understand how that happens, you have to go to the moment the original airbag is gone.

## 10:52 Why the Replacement Part Matters

An airbag only works once. After it deploys in a crash, it's used up — and if the car is going to be driven again, the airbag system has to be repaired and the deployed parts replaced.

That moment — the replacement — is where this story lives.

According to NBC's January 2026 report, investigators say these inflators were ending up in American cars most often after a previous crash — when the original airbag was replaced with a cheap substitute. Reporting on NHTSA's April findings described the airbags in question as "replacement units installed during repair."

And NBC's report included a detail that narrows the picture further. According to NHTSA, all of the known deaths at that point involved Chevrolet Malibus and Hyundai Sonatas "with salvage or rebuilt titles."

If you've never owned one, a salvage or rebuilt title is a mark in a car's paperwork showing that it suffered serious damage at some point and was later put back on the road. These cars can be perfectly legal to drive. They're often cheaper. And for a lot of buyers, cheaper is the whole point.

So follow the chain. A car is badly damaged. Its airbags deploy. Someone takes it on as a repair. The repair has many expensive line items — and one of them is the airbag system. Somewhere in that process, someone chooses a part. If that choice is a cheap, substandard inflator, the car can leave the shop looking perfect. The steering wheel can look new. And the next owner has no reason to suspect anything.

That last point isn't speculation. NBC's report put it bluntly: drivers "often have no idea what airbags are inside their car until it's too late."

Where were these parts coming from? One person interviewed in that report said: "Somehow these inflators are being imported into the United States. And they're being attached to airbag modules and being sold, we think, through online marketplaces."

Notice the words "we think." Even the people describing the problem on camera presented the sales channel as a belief, not a mapped supply chain.

That's what makes the replacement moment so different from the factory. At the factory, there's a bill of materials. The automaker knows which supplier built which part, which cars it went into, and how to find those cars again. After a crash, in a repair, a replacement part can come from almost anywhere — and whatever record exists may sit in one shop's files, or nowhere at all.

And think about who ends up holding the risk. Not necessarily the person whose car crashed the first time — they may have moved on long ago. Not necessarily the person who did the repair — they may not have known where the part came from. Very often, it's the next owner: someone who bought a used car, maybe a cheap one, maybe their first, with every reason to believe the airbag in front of them is the one that came with the car.

That's why NBC's report framed the January warning this way: it "isn't just for used car shoppers, it's for anyone driving a used vehicle, especially one that's been in a crash before."

So who made these parts? And what does the company say for itself?

## 14:44 DTN

The company linked to these inflators is based in China's Jilin Province. In English-language reporting, its name appears in more than one form — "Jilin Province Detiannuo Safety Technology" in some reports, "Jilin Province Detiannuo Automobile Safety System" in others. Everyone shortens it the same way: DTN.

Here is what DTN says. According to reporting in January 2026, the company's homepage reads: "We do not do business with the United States, and any of our products are prohibited from being sold to the United States." NBC reported the same thing — that DTN "even says on its own website its products are not allowed to be sold in the United States."

NBC said it reached out to the company for comment and had not heard back.

So, on one side: a company saying publicly that its products don't belong in the U.S. market. On the other: a U.S. safety agency saying inflators made by that company are in American cars, linked to deaths.

Both of those things can be true at the same time. A manufacturer can say it doesn't sell to a country, and its products — or products carrying its name — can still arrive there through other hands. What the public record we were able to verify does not tell us is exactly how each part traveled, or who handled it along the way. And we're not going to fill that gap with guesses.

There is one more piece of the public record worth showing you — with a clear warning label on it. On YouTube, there's a channel presenting itself as DTN. It's titled "DTN Air bag inflator," with product videos going back to 2016. The video descriptions list "Jilin Province Detiannuo Automobile Safety System Co., Ltd," a city in Jilin Province, and a link to a storefront on Alibaba, the global wholesale marketplace.

What does that show? That years before these deaths, products under that name were being marketed online, in English, through a global wholesale platform.

What doesn't it show? It doesn't prove the channel is officially run by the company. It doesn't prove any inflator in any American car came through that storefront. And it doesn't contradict the company's statement that it doesn't sell to the United States. It's context — not a conclusion.

Now read the government's own words slowly. In its April findings, as reported at the time, NHTSA said the inflators were "likely illegally imported into the United States by unknown importers."

Likely. Unknown.

Those two words are the whole problem. "Likely illegally imported" means the agency believes they shouldn't have come in through any legitimate channel — without naming who brought them. "Unknown importers" means there is no company at the border end of this chain that the government can point to and say: you — recall these.

Behind the numbers are real people. Reporting in October 2025 described one case: a 22-year-old mother of two in Florida who died in a crash. According to a lawsuit her family filed against DTN, she was traveling at about 30 miles per hour when an airbag with a DTN inflator exploded. That's an allegation in a lawsuit, not a court finding we could verify. But it describes exactly the kind of crash — around thirty miles an hour — that airbags exist to make survivable.

So here is where the investigation leaves things. A manufacturer that says it doesn't sell here. Importers the agency says it hasn't identified. Parts installed after the cars left the factory, during repairs that no central database tracks.

Which leads to the question at the center of this video. Why can't the recall system just find these parts and pull them out?

## 19:03 Why the Recall System Can't Simply Find It

Let's start with how the system is supposed to work, in NHTSA's own words to the public.

"Find out if your vehicle has a Takata recall. Use the VIN lookup tool at NHTSA.gov. Or download the SaferCar app. Repairs are free."

That's from a public service ad NHTSA released on September 24, 2026. An older NHTSA explainer puts it the same way: enter your vehicle identification number — your VIN — sign up for recall alerts, and if you have a recall, contact your local dealer to get it fixed for free.

Notice the shape of that system. It starts with a vehicle. A specific car, with a specific VIN.

Another NHTSA ad, released the same day, compresses the whole idea into a single sentence: "Your vehicle is either affected by safety recalls, or it isn't."

Either it is, or it isn't. Hold on to that sentence — we're going to come back to it.

And what counts as a safety defect in the first place? NHTSA's own explainer defines it as "a problem with your vehicle that poses an unreasonable risk to your safety that could result in a crash and injury or death." It can be a problem with "the vehicle's materials, how the vehicle's built, or how it performs." And when the agency lists examples, it names three: "brake failure, steering failure, or exploding airbags."

Exploding airbags are one of NHTSA's own textbook examples of a safety defect. Remember the phrase "unreasonable risk" — it's going to come up again, in a letter, in a much bigger fight.

For a recall to appear when you type in your VIN, someone first has to know which vehicles are affected. With a factory defect, that's usually possible: the automaker built the car, knows which supplier provided which part, and can tie a production range to a list of VINs. That list is what makes the free fix work. Find the cars, send the letters, fix them at the dealer.

Now apply that to a DTN inflator. There's no automaker who installed it. The company linked to it says it doesn't sell here. NHTSA says the importers are unknown. And the part went in during a repair, after the car left the factory. So which list of VINs would a recall be built on?

There's also no reliable count to start from. Reporting on the January warning said NHTSA's investigation covered an estimated ten thousand inflators. But how many are actually installed in American cars was, as an earlier report put it, unclear.

That's why NHTSA's advice on these inflators doesn't sound like a normal recall notice. In its January warning, as quoted by reporters, the agency said: "NHTSA urges used vehicle owners and buyers to learn their vehicle's history and ensure the vehicle has genuine air bag inflators." And: "If an owner or buyer does not know the vehicle's history, they should get a history report and have the vehicle inspected by a reputable mechanic or dealership if it has been in a previous crash with air bag deployment."

Learn the history. Get it inspected. That isn't "check your VIN." It's "go find out yourself."

NBC's report added a practical point from experts: if you're concerned, take the car to a mechanic — they can check who manufactured the airbag. And if a DTN inflator is found, NHTSA's advice was not to drive the car until the airbag is replaced with one that meets U.S. safety standards.

What about the ban? In early April 2026, NHTSA said it was considering a nationwide ban on the sale and import of these inflators. We were not able to review a final order ourselves, so we're not going to describe its legal status as fact. Check NHTSA's website for where it stands today.

But think about what any ban, at its strongest, is built to do. A ban is aimed at the future — at parts sold or imported from that point forward. On its own, it doesn't reach into a car that had one of these inflators installed last year. Those parts are already in steering wheels. Finding them still depends on the same thing: someone looking.

And here's a detail that adds weight to all of this. On NHTSA's own YouTube channel, there's a video whose title reads "NHTSA Airbag Counterfeit Final." It was published in October 2012. There's no transcript available, so we won't tell you what it says. But the title and the date tell you something by themselves: concern about non-genuine replacement airbags is not new. The agency had a video on the subject fourteen years ago.

So the DTN case is less a surprise than a stress test. It shows what happens when a known weak spot — the replacement airbag — meets a part with no accountable source. A recall system built around manufacturers and VINs has very little to grab on to.

But what happens when there is something to grab on to? When the company is known, the part was installed at the factory, and the government puts its demand in writing? You'd expect that to be the easy case.

It wasn't.

## 25:07 ARC and Delphi: The Company That Said No

May 2023. In a letter, NHTSA demands that a Tennessee-based company, ARC Automotive, recall 67 million airbag inflators.

Sixty-seven million.

The agency said those inflators posed "an unreasonable risk of death or injury" and could rupture and project metal fragments.

There's that phrase again: unreasonable risk. The same words NHTSA uses to define a safety defect. In this letter, the agency wasn't describing a possible problem. It was invoking its own definition. According to ABC News, the inflators in question were manufactured before 2018, and at least twelve automakers had used ARC components in their airbags.

To put that number in perspective, one person NBC interviewed noted there were about 284 million vehicles in the United States — and said a recall of 67 million inflators would be one of the largest ever in the country. ABC framed it another way: a recall that could ultimately affect nearly a quarter of the cars on American roads.

And then ARC said no.

In a letter dated May 11, 2023, according to ABC, the company denied its products were defective. ARC said any problems "resulted from random one-off manufacturing anomalies that were properly addressed with individual recalls." As NBC reported it, ARC argued that "just seven incidents in the U.S. over a decade and a half do not support a finding that a systemic and prevalent defect exists across this population."

That's the core of ARC's position, and it deserves to be stated fairly. In a population of tens of millions of parts, a handful of failures across fifteen years is — statistically — rare.

NHTSA didn't claim otherwise. As NBC summarized it, the agency acknowledged that while incidents are rare, the ones that have occurred have been severe — including a driver's death in Michigan in 2021. According to ABC, regulators pointed to at least nine airbag-related incidents between 2009 and March 2023, resulting in two deaths and multiple injuries.

So here's the disagreement in its simplest form. ARC: isolated anomalies, already handled. NHTSA: rare, but severe enough to require recalling the whole population.

This is one of the hardest kinds of safety decision there is. Recall tens of millions of parts because of a handful of failures, and you impose an enormous cost — and you'd better be right that the failures share a cause. Don't recall them, and if they do share a cause, every future failure becomes one you were warned about. In a sense, both sides were arguing about the same question: what does "rare" mean, when the outcome can be a death?

Meanwhile, one automaker didn't wait for the argument to end. General Motors announced it was voluntarily recalling nearly one million vehicles equipped with ARC inflators — certain 2014 through 2017 Chevrolet Traverse, GMC Acadia and Buick Enclave models — a step GM said it was taking "out of an abundance of caution." According to ABC, owners would receive letters starting June 25, 2023, and dealers would replace the front driver airbag module.

What can a regulator do when a manufacturer refuses? ABC laid out the path at the time: NHTSA would schedule a public hearing, and it could then take ARC to court to force a recall.

September 2023. NHTSA moves forward. And the number changes. The formal push now covered 52 million inflators — made by ARC, and by a second manufacturer, Delphi. The agency said ruptured inflators from them had killed at least two people and injured at least seven since 2009.

Why 67 million in May and 52 million in September? The sources we were able to review don't explain the difference, so we won't guess. What we can tell you is that these were two different steps: a demand letter in May, and a formal action in September.

Consumer Reports told ABC's Philadelphia station that this push for a recall and a hearing was unusual for the agency — and that "they're only taking this step because they feel very confident in the data and the analysis that they've done."

ARC's position hadn't moved. The company disputed the findings, saying its extensive field testing did not find any defect.

The vehicles involved, that report said, reportedly came from General Motors, Ford, Tesla, Toyota, Hyundai, Kia, Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Volkswagen. But at that point, the exact vehicles had not yet been publicly identified by NHTSA.

And the second name — Delphi — has its own explanation. According to Autoline, the industry news program, of those 52 million inflators, about 11 million were made by Delphi, a company now owned by Autoliv, under a licensing agreement with ARC. In other words, the case reached beyond a single company.

On October 5, 2023, NHTSA held a public meeting on the case. Autoline reported that the agency "again argued that 52 million airbag inflators made by ARC and Delphi Automotive need to be recalled because they're defective" — and that ARC disagreed that there was a safety defect and had so far refused.

That same report laid out what would come next if the agency went further. If NHTSA ordered a recall, ARC could challenge it in federal court. And NHTSA, for its part, could go to court to force an automaker to comply.

So now you can see the full shape of the standoff. Not a press release and a quick fix — a formal, contested, legal process, with both sides holding a path to a courtroom.

One more note on the numbers, because precision matters here. Reports at the time described the human toll slightly differently. Autoline described seven injuries and one death in the United States. Other reports, citing the agency, described at least two deaths and at least seven injuries since 2009 — and NBC specifically mentioned a driver's death in Michigan in 2021. So the conservative version is this: at least one death in the United States, and at least two deaths cited by regulators overall.

And that is where the public record we could verify gets thin. We could not confirm that a final order requiring ARC and Delphi to recall those inflators has ever been issued. We're not saying it never happened. We're saying we couldn't document it — so we won't claim it either way.

Which brings us back to the lookup tool. Your VIN check will show you GM's recall if your vehicle is part of it. It can't show you a recall that doesn't exist.

Put the two cases side by side. DTN: no company accepts the part — so no one stands behind it. ARC: the company is known, the part was installed at the factory, the government put its demand in writing — and the company said the evidence didn't support it. In one case, there's no one to argue with. In the other, there's someone to argue with — and the argument itself takes time.

And then there's the third case. The one where the recall happened. Where nobody is arguing anymore. Where the fix is free.

And it still isn't finished.

## 33:24 Takata: The Recall That Never Finished

If you've heard of one airbag recall, it's probably Takata.

NHTSA describes it in a public service video like this: "There are millions of defective Takata air bags on the road that need repair." The agency explains that the problem is with the chemical inside the airbag that inflates it — and that, in a crash, a recalled Takata airbag "can explode, sending sharp metal fragments flying at 200 miles per hour."

That's the chemistry failure from our framework. And NHTSA is specific about who faces the most risk: "The greatest risk is in older vehicles, especially vehicles driven in hot, humid areas."

Think about what "older vehicles" means in practice. Every year that passes, the cars still carrying unrepaired Takata airbags get older. Older cars change hands — from first owners to second, third, fourth. They get cheaper. And the people driving them may be the least likely to have received, or kept, the original recall letter.

That's why, over time, the warnings have escalated — from "get this fixed" to "stop driving." In May 2023, a "do not drive" warning went out for about 90,000 BMW vehicles built between 2000 and 2006, including 3 Series and 5 Series models. These cars were already under a Takata recall. What changed was the urgency. As ABC reported it, regulators said they were "becoming more dangerous by the day." The warning itself described the risk to occupants as "dire," and said these airbags had "an extremely high probability of failure during a crash." Parts, repair and towing: all free.

Free — and still not done. That's the pattern you're going to see again and again in this chapter.

In Texas, a Dallas TV station's consumer reporter has followed the same thread. According to WFAA, Carfax had identified Texas as leading the country in the number of vehicles still on the road with recalled Takata airbags. And WFAA reported NHTSA's explanation of why that matters, in three ingredients: high temperatures, environmental moisture and humidity, and the passage of time — which is why affected vehicles there were designated a top priority for repairs.

Then WFAA told the story of one viewer. In 2025, his 2013 car still had a recalled Takata airbag. When he checked, he got a message: repair not yet available. Years into the recall. He asked for an estimate and a loaner car. That went nowhere — until he filed a formal complaint with NHTSA and asked the manufacturer, in writing, for an explanation. At that point, according to WFAA, the dealer took the car in and gave him a loaner while they waited for the part — at no cost.

Notice what it took. Not a recall letter. Not a VIN lookup. A complaint to a federal agency and a written request to the manufacturer. For one owner, persistence closed the gap. For millions of others, the question is whether anyone pushes at all.

February 2026. Stellantis — the company behind Chrysler, Dodge, Jeep and Ram — issues a "do not drive" warning for about 225,000 vehicles with unrepaired Takata airbags. The affected models span model years 2003 through 2016, including certain Dodge Rams, Chargers, Challengers, Durangos, Chrysler 300s and Jeep Wranglers.

As one TV report put it: "This isn't just a recall. It's a do not drive warning."

That same report noted that at least 28 people have died, and hundreds more have been injured, because of these airbags.

And the 225,000 is only one company's share. That same week, WPXI in Pittsburgh reported on a "do not drive" notice from Carfax — and said there were still about five million cars nationwide with unrepaired Takata airbags. WPXI's own reporting found California, Texas and Florida at the top of the list of states with the most unrepaired vehicles.

Now put those two pieces side by side. NHTSA: the greatest risk is in older vehicles in hot, humid areas. The states with the most unrepaired cars, by that reporting: California, Texas, Florida.

And the fix? Free. "If you have a recall, contact your local dealer to get it fixed for free." "Your local dealership or the manufacturer will fix the issue for free."

So this is the strangest of the three cases. With DTN, no one stands behind the part. With ARC, the recall turned into a standoff. With Takata, the recall exists, the fix costs nothing, the lookup tool works — and millions of cars, by that estimate, still carry the defective part. The failure isn't in the engineering anymore. It's in the last steps: reaching the owner, having the part, and getting the car to the dealer.

Which brings us to the present. On September 24, 2026, NHTSA released a new set of public service ads about Takata airbags. One of them shows a family in a car — kids playing a game, a dad saying hello — and then a voiceover: "Air bags are designed to protect you. Recalled Takata air bags don't."

A safety agency is still producing new ads, in 2026, asking people to check for a recall that many of them probably think of as history.

And there's a connection here that's easy to miss. The cars most likely to still carry an unrepaired Takata airbag — older, cheaper, passed through several owners — live in the same part of the market as the cars most likely to have received a cheap replacement part after a crash. Different failures. Often, the same buyers.

## 39:45 The Systemic Question

Let's step back and look at all three together.

Takata. A known manufacturer, a recall that exists, a free fix — and, by Carfax's estimate as reported, millions of cars still not repaired. The assumption that broke: that once a recall exists, people will get it done.

ARC and Delphi. Known manufacturers, parts installed at the factory, a federal demand in writing — and a company that said the evidence didn't support a recall. The assumption that broke: that a regulator's conclusion quickly becomes a fix. As far as we could document, it didn't.

DTN. A part with no accountable seller, likely illegally imported, NHTSA says, by unknown importers, installed during repairs. The assumption that broke: that every part in a car can be traced back to someone responsible for it.

Three different failures. But look at what they share. In every case, the critical information — is this part safe, has it been fixed, where did it come from — doesn't travel with the car.

The airbag is one of the few safety systems you can't check yourself. You can see your tire tread. You can feel your brakes. You can hear your engine. But you can't look at your steering wheel and know what's inside it. So the whole system rests on paperwork: build records, recall lists, repair histories. When the paperwork is complete, the system works. When it isn't, the risk lands on whoever is sitting in the driver's seat.

Now, an important counterpoint — because it matters for your safety. None of this means airbags are dangerous. NHTSA's own messaging is direct: airbags "are designed to protect you." "Make sure the airbag in your vehicle is there to save your life, not take it." The failures in this story involve specific parts: recalled Takata inflators, ARC and Delphi inflators the government said can rupture, and substandard replacement inflators like DTN's. Disabling an airbag — or avoiding a car because it has one — is not the lesson. Knowing which part is in your car is the lesson.

And a second counterpoint, in fairness to everyone involved. Rare is real. ARC's argument — a handful of incidents across tens of millions of parts — is a real statistical argument. The agency's answer — rare, but severe — is also real. What's hard is that the person who bought the used car never gets to choose which side of that argument they end up on.

## 45:19 What You Can Actually Do

Here's the practical part. Everything below comes from NHTSA's guidance or the reporting we've cited — and none of it requires you to open or touch anything yourself.

Step one: run the recall check. NHTSA's own reminder puts it plainly: "Your vehicle could have an open safety recall, and you might not know it." Go to NHTSA.gov and search using your VIN or your license plate. You can use NHTSA's SaferCar app and sign up for recall alerts. That catches recalls that exist — including Takata, and including GM's ARC-related recall if your vehicle is part of it.

Step two: if the lookup shows an open Takata recall — especially one with a "do not drive" warning — don't drive the car until it's repaired. The repair is free.

Step three: know your car's history. This is the step that covers what a recall check can't. NHTSA's guidance, as quoted in January 2026: if you don't know the vehicle's history, get a history report — and if the car has been in a previous crash with airbag deployment, have it inspected by a reputable mechanic or dealership. According to NHTSA, as reported by NBC, this applies with extra force to cars with salvage or rebuilt titles.

Step four: if you're shopping for a used car, do that research before you buy. NHTSA urged buyers to get a vehicle history report before purchasing any used car.

Step five: let a professional check the airbag. Experts told NBC that a mechanic can check who manufactured the airbag in your car. Don't try to open or inspect airbag components yourself — they contain the energetic materials we talked about earlier, and this is a job for someone trained to handle them.

Step six: if a DTN inflator is found, don't drive the car until the airbag is replaced with one that meets U.S. safety standards.

Step seven: report it. NHTSA asked people to report suspected illegal airbags to the agency. For any suspected safety defect, the agency says to report it at NHTSA.gov/recalls — and says, "We review every complaint we receive." And according to reporting on the January warning, drivers who find one of these DTN inflators were advised to contact Homeland Security or the FBI.

And if a fix is listed but "not yet available," don't assume that's the end of it. As the Texas case showed, a formal complaint to NHTSA and a written request to the manufacturer can move things.

And one more, for the case we couldn't fully resolve. If your car comes from one of the brands reportedly linked to ARC or Delphi inflators, and your VIN check shows no open recall, there isn't a free repair to schedule today. What you can do is keep checking — and sign up for alerts, so that if anything changes, you hear about it.

## 48:39 Final: The Part You Only Test Once

Let's go back to where we started.

"Minor crashes that you should walk away from."

That phrase is the entire idea behind an airbag. The crash still happens. The car still gets wrecked. But the person walks away.

In this story, that promise breaks in three different ways. A chemical that changes as the car ages. A container that the government says can rupture and the manufacturer says is sound. And a replacement part that nobody will stand behind, installed after a crash, in a car that looks perfectly normal.

The first one has a recall, a free fix, and an agency still producing new ads in 2026 asking people to use it. The second one, as far as we could document, never reached a clear ending. And the third barely fits the recall system at all — because the part went in after the cars left the factory.

Remember the sentence from NHTSA's ad? "Your vehicle is either affected by safety recalls, or it isn't."

For Takata, that sentence works. There's a recall; your car is in it or it isn't. For the ARC and Delphi inflators outside the recalls that were issued, the honest answer is: we couldn't document a final decision. And for a substandard inflator installed after a crash, the sentence barely applies — because the lookup is built around your car's VIN, and this part went in after the car left the factory. What matters is the part itself — and whatever is, or isn't, written down about where it came from.

What connects them isn't a company or a country. It's information. Where a part came from. Whether it was ever fixed. What happened to the car before you owned it. In every one of these cases, that information existed somewhere — in a factory record, a recall list, a repair shop, a seller's listing. It just never reached the person in the driver's seat.

So tonight, do the part the system can't do for you. Check your VIN. Learn your car's history. And if it's ever been in a crash where the airbags went off, find out what's in the steering wheel now.

Because an airbag is something you only ever get to test once.
