# CHAPTER 2 — WHAT'S ACTUALLY INSIDE AN AIRBAG (06:55)

[VOICEOVER]
Most people picture an airbag as a balloon. A fabric bag that inflates. That's the part you see.

The part that matters for this story is the one you don't see. It's called the inflator.

[VISUAL DIRECTION]
High-speed slow-motion lab footage of an airbag deploying from a steering wheel on a test rig; then a small cylindrical metal inflator resting on a mechanic's workbench.

[VOICEOVER]
An inflator is, essentially, a small gas generator. Inside it are chemicals that ignite during a collision to rapidly fill the airbag with gas. NHTSA, explaining the Takata recall, describes the problem in exactly those terms: "The problem is with a chemical inside the airbag that works to inflate the airbag during a crash."

And it has to happen fast. As one person NBC interviewed put it, the airbag "has to deploy really quickly — the blink of an eye."

[VISUAL DIRECTION]
Gloved hands measuring a generic airbag inflator with digital calipers on a white lab bench, high-speed camera rig in the background, clinical light.

[VOICEOVER]
Think about what that asks of a small metal part. A sealed container has to go from inert to full of rapidly expanding gas almost instantly — and then send that gas, in a controlled way, into a fabric bag in front of a person's face and chest. The bag is the cushion. The container is what keeps all that energy pointed where it's supposed to go.

When it works, that container is invisible. NHTSA's own public service ads say it simply: "Air bags are designed to protect you."

When it doesn't work, the container itself becomes the hazard.

[VISUAL DIRECTION]
Extreme macro of a metal inflator canister, light gleaming along its seam, dark background; cut to a calm, empty car cabin with the airbag cover intact.

[VOICEOVER]
NHTSA has described that failure, for one group of inflators, as an inflator that "may rupture when the vehicle's airbag is commanded to deploy, causing metal debris to be forcefully ejected into the passenger compartment." For the DTN inflators, one person interviewed in NBC's January report described it this way: in a crash — "even a minor crash" — these substandard inflators were "deploying and overpressuring," sending metal toward the driver.

Now here's the framework that ties this entire story together. An inflator can fail for different reasons — and each reason exposes a different weak point in the system around it.

One: the chemistry. The material inside behaves differently over time than it did on the day it was built. That's the Takata story. NHTSA says the greatest risk is in older vehicles, especially vehicles driven in hot, humid areas.

Two: the container. The inflator was built by a known manufacturer and installed at the factory — and the government says some of them can rupture. The manufacturer says those were isolated anomalies. That's the ARC story, and as you'll see, it turned into a standoff.

This is one of the hardest kinds of safety decision there is. Don't recall them, and if they do share a cause, every future failure becomes one you were warned about. # CHAPTER 8 — THE SYSTEMIC QUESTION (39:45)

[VOICEOVER]
Let's step back and look at all three together.

Takata. A known manufacturer, a recall that exists, a free fix — and, by Carfax's estimate as reported, millions of cars still not repaired. The assumption that broke: that once a recall exists, people will get it done.

ARC and Delphi. Known manufacturers, parts installed at the factory, a federal demand in writing — and a company that said the evidence didn't support a recall. The assumption that broke: that a regulator's conclusion quickly becomes a fix. As far as we could document, it didn't.

DTN. A part with no accountable seller, likely illegally imported, NHTSA says, by unknown importers, installed during repairs. The assumption that broke: that every part in a car can be traced back to someone responsible for it.

[VISUAL DIRECTION]
Callback: the three inflators on a dark table under spotlights — weathered, factory-packaged, unmarked — each lit in turn.

[VOICEOVER]
Three different failures. But look at what they share. In every case, the critical information — is this part safe, has it been fixed, where did it come from — doesn't travel with the car.

The airbag is one of the few safety systems you can't check yourself. You can see your tire tread. You can feel your brakes. You can hear your engine. But you can't look at your steering wheel and know what's inside it. So the whole system rests on paperwork: build records, recall lists, repair histories. When the paperwork is complete, the system works. When it isn't, the risk lands on whoever is sitting in the driver's seat.

[VISUAL DIRECTION]
Macro shots of tire tread, a brake rotor and an engine bay; then a closed, opaque steering wheel hub.

"Make sure the airbag in your vehicle is there to save your life, not take it." The failures in this story involve specific parts: recalled Takata inflators, ARC and Delphi inflators the government said can rupture, and substandard replacement inflators like DTN's.
